## 1. Two actions: Vienna and London

- Johann Andreas Stein (1728-1792), Augsburg, developed the Viennese action, the *Prellmechanik*, "perhaps as early as 1769",
  refined through the 1770s; the hammer sits on the key itself, with no intermediate lever. Stein is credited with knee-lever
  controls for the dampers. Late-18th-century pianos: "five octaves (sixty-one keys)"; "a full seven octaves (eighty-five keys)
  by the middle of the century". Anton Walter worked in Vienna from the early 1780s on Stein's model; Mozart bought a Walter
  about 1783; it is in the Mozart Museum in Salzburg. Mozart visited Stein in 1777 and praised his pianos.
  Source: The Met, "The Piano: Viennese Instruments", https://www.metmuseum.org/essays/the-piano-viennese-instruments
- Viennese action: a "pleasant light elastic touch" (Dolge), the action of the pianos played by Haydn, Mozart and Beethoven.
  English action: Cristofori's kind of action taken to London by Zumpe and Backers; Backers' action patented 1776, refined by
  Stodart (1777) and Broadwood (1780). Erard's double escapement with the repetition lever "patented in 1808 and 1821";
  the repetition lever "facilitated rapidly repeating notes".
  Source: Wikipedia, "Action (piano)", https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Action_(piano)

## 2. The keyboard grows (compass)

| A0-C8 (A''-c5) | 88 | modern instruments | |

- An Erard of 1836: "Six octaves and a fourth, CC - f4" (C1-F7). Liszt "preferred Erard's more dramatic range of tone colors";
  Mendelssohn "owned and was pleased with Erard pianos"; Chopin "played both makes, preferring the more intimate sound of the
  Pleyel". Source: Frederick Historic Piano Collection, https://frederickcollection.org/Erard1836.html
- "The keyboard compass was gradually extended from five to five-and-a-half octaves after 1810, and six and a half after
  1820." Source: Fortepian (Chopin Institute site), https://fortepian.instrumenty.edu.pl/en/history/the-piano-in-the-1st-half-of-the-19th-century
  (The room gives compasses as note names and key counts, from the table above, not as octave words.)

## 3. The pedals

Source unless noted: Wikipedia, "Piano pedals", https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Piano_pedals
- Early pianos: a hand stop raised the dampers (Zumpe's London square, 1767: two hand stops, bass and treble). In Germany a
  knee lever replaced the hand stop about 1765; "virtually all the fortepianos of the last three decades of the eighteenth
  century were equipped with a knee lever" (Crombie). Mozart praised Stein's knee levers about 1777; his own Walter
  (c. 1782-1785) had two knee levers. Backers' 1772 grand is probably the first piano with pedals. A Streicher grand, Vienna
  1814, had both knee levers and pedals. "After 1810 pedals replaced knee levers" (Fortepian, above).
- The moderator: a strip of soft cloth or leather between hammers and strings; popular on Viennese pianos; Mozart's Walter had
  a moderator stop, Beethoven's 1803 Erard had one.
- Una corda: Cristofori's invention; on his pianos a hand stop shifted the action so the hammers struck one string instead of
  two. Late-18th-century pianos (three strings a note) let the player choose tre, due or una corda by how far the pedal went
  down. On a modern grand the treble hammers strike two strings instead of three. On an upright the left pedal is not a true
  una corda: it moves the hammers closer to the strings. (Henle: on a grand "the keyboard and striking mechanism shift
  slightly right"; release marked tre corde or tutte le corde; Beethoven used it repeatedly, in the late sonatas as part of
  the composition. https://blog.henle.de/en/2013/06/24/una-corda-%e2%80%93-con-sord-%e2%80%93-mit-verschiebung-how-do-i-%e2%80%98mute%e2%80%99-my-piano/)
- Senza sordino in the "Moonlight" sonata: the right pedal, which lifts the dampers (Henle, above).
- Sostenuto: first shown by Boisselot & Fils, Paris exposition 1844; Albert Steinway perfected and patented it in 1874.
- Earliest pedal markings: a Beethoven sketch of 1790-1792 marks chords "with the knee"; his first pedal marking in a score is
  in his first two piano concertos (1795); Haydn first specified pedal in 1794; Beethoven: nearly 800 pedal indications.

## 4. Iron, felt and the singing tone

Source: Fortepian (Chopin Institute site), above.
- Babcock patented an iron frame for the square piano in 1825; Broadwood patented iron bars with a metal string plate in 1827;
  Chickering patented the first one-piece iron frame for the grand in 1843.
- Henri Pape patented hammer felt in France in 1826; "more conservative Viennese makers covered felt hammer heads with an
  additional layer of leather"; felt prevailed in the second half of the century.
- Erard's double escapement, patented 1821, "proved to be the best, and is used till today in a modified form".
