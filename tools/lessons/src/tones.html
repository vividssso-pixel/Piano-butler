Tones and semitones

A semitone is a half step, to the very next key. A tone is a whole step: two half steps. Two signs move a note by a half step: the sharp up, the flat down.

Written by Clara Sohyun Park · Updated 13 October 2026

From the teacher

I start with the white keys. E to F and B to C are already half steps, all by themselves, with no black key in between. Once a student has seen that, half steps and whole steps make sense everywhere on the piano.

Then I ask: who is your nearest neighbour? On the piano, a key's nearest neighbour is the very next key, black or white. That is a half step. Step past one neighbour and you have a whole step.

A half step: a semitone

A half step is the smallest step on the piano: from a key to its nearest neighbour, black or white.

Its other name is a semitone (semi means half).

A half step goes up to the right or down to the left.

{{svg:half-step}}

From D, both half steps land on black keys. From a black key, both land on white keys.

From C, play every key, black and white, up to the next C: 12 half steps. This walk is the chromatic scale.

A whole step: a tone

Two half steps make a whole step, with one key in between. Its other name is a tone. C to D is a whole step, over the black key. Two black keys with one white key between them are a whole step too.

{{svg:whole-step}}

The white half steps: E F and B C

Walk up the white keys letter by letter. Most steps have a black key in between: whole steps. Twice there is none.

E ends the small house and F starts the big house. B ends the big house and C starts the next small house. E F and B C are only a half step apart.

{{svg:white-half-steps}}

From C up to the next C, the white keys go whole, whole, half, whole, whole, whole, half. That order is the major scale's recipe. The Scales chapter builds scales with it.

Sharp and flat: one key, two names

The sharp sign ♯ moves a note up a half step. The flat sign ♭ moves it down a half step.

A black key is a hidden attic shared by the neighbours on each side. From Clara's C, it is C sharp, C♯. From the Dog's D, it is D flat, D♭. One attic, two names.

Every black key works the same way: C♯ = D♭, D♯ = E♭, F♯ = G♭, G♯ = A♭, A♯ = B♭.

{{svg:sharp-flat}}

Between E and F there is no attic, so E sharp is the white key F. A sharp or a flat is not always a black key.

Sharps and flats on the staff

On the staff, the sign goes just before the note, on the same line or space as its head. The sharp's little box and the flat's round belly sit on it.

Say the letter first: "F sharp", "B flat". Write the sign first.

My five checks

Show each one at the piano.

Steps

  • I can play a half step (a semitone) up and down from any key
  • I can play a whole step (a tone) up and down from any key
  • I can show the white half steps: E F and B C

Sharps and flats

  • I can name every black key two ways: C♯ = D♭
  • I can read sharps and flats on the staff and play them

Common questions

What is a semitone on the piano?

A semitone is the smallest step on the piano: to the very next key, black or white. Its other name is a half step. Twelve semitones take you from one C to the next.

What is the difference between a tone and a semitone?

A tone (a whole step) is two semitones (two half steps), with one key in between. C to D is a tone: the black key C sharp sits between them. E to F is a semitone: no key sits between them.

Why are E to F and B to C semitones?

Because there is no black key between them. Every other pair of white neighbours has one, so it is a tone. That is why the white keys from C to C go whole, whole, half, whole, whole, whole, half.

Why does a black key have two names?

A black key can be reached from either side. A half step up from C, it is C sharp. A half step down from D, it is D flat. One key, like an attic shared by the neighbours on each side.

Is a sharp always a black key?

No. A sharp is the very next key up, black or white. So E sharp is the white key F, and C flat is B. Beginners meet sharps and flats mostly on black keys. The white-key names come later, with scales.
